The Schrödinger equation, from the beginning

This is the rule that matter follows at the scale of atoms. You need no physics background. Each idea comes with something you can turn, drag or run.

The puzzle

Throw a ball and Newton's laws tell you where it goes. Know its position and speed now and you can work out its whole path.

Electrons do not behave like that. Send them one at a time at a wall with two narrow slits, and record where each one lands on a screen behind it. Every electron arrives at a single spot, like a tiny ball. But the spots pile up into stripes, and stripes are what overlapping waves make.

So something wave-like travels and something particle-like arrives. Quantum mechanics keeps both, with three ideas:

  1. Every particle has a wave, called its wavefunction.
  2. The Schrödinger equation says how that wave moves.
  3. Where the wave is strong, the particle is likely to be found.

The wave: ψ

The wavefunction is written ψ, the Greek letter psi. A water wave has a height at every point. ψ has an arrow at every point: something with a length and a direction, like a clock hand. The mathematical name for such an arrow is a complex number.

  • The arrow's length is the amplitude. Square it and you get the chance of finding the particle at that point.
  • The arrow's direction is the phase. Colour shows phase: the full colour wheel in the moving figures, and two colours in the atom at the end.
  • How quickly the arrows turn as you move along the line is the momentum. A tight twist means a short wavelength and a fast particle.

The equation

In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger started from the ordinary rule for energy: total energy is energy of motion plus energy of position. In symbols, E = p²/2m + V. He rewrote each piece in the language of waves, using two relations between particles and waves. Both were established for light, and de Broglie had proposed that matter obeys them too.

In words: the landscape sets how fast each arrow spins in place, while how sharply the wave curves relative to its neighbours changes each arrow's direction and length—moving probability around while keeping the total fixed. That is the whole law.

The equation never says where the particle is. It says how the odds change. Press Look for the particle: you find it at one spot, picked at random, more often where the hill is tall. The wave then starts again from a narrow packet at that spot. Try it several times. You get a different answer each time, and that randomness is part of nature as far as anyone can tell.

Trapped waves and energy levels

A guitar string is fixed at both ends, so it can only vibrate in shapes that fit: one hump, two humps, three. A particle shut in a box is the same. Only certain wave shapes fit between the walls, each shape has its own energy, and no energy in between is possible. This is the "quantum" in quantum mechanics.

That is why atoms give off light of particular colours only. An electron bound to an atom has a ladder of allowed energies. When it drops from one rung to another, the light it emits carries exactly the difference.

Three dimensions: the atom

Real space has three dimensions. The equation keeps its form: the wave can now curve in three directions, and all three count towards the energy of motion. For hydrogen, the landscape V is the electrical pull of the proton on the electron. The stationary states of that problem are the shapes below. The clouds do not change in time. Every dot is a place you might find the electron if you looked. Where the dots are dense it is likely to be, and the gaps are places it is never found. These shapes decide how atoms bond, so they are where chemistry starts.

What to remember

  • ψ is a wave made of arrows. An arrow's length squared is the chance of finding the particle there.
  • The Schrödinger equation updates the arrows from two things—the curvature of the wave and the height of the landscape—shifting probability from place to place while keeping the total at 100%.
  • Waves add. That gives the stripes behind two slits and the sloshing of a mixed state.
  • A trapped wave fits only in certain shapes, so a trapped particle has only certain energies.
  • The equation gives odds. Looking gives one result.

Every term, in one place

Wavefunction, ψ
The wave that goes with a particle: one arrow at every point in space, changing in time.
Complex number
A number with a size and a direction. An arrow.
Amplitude
The length of the arrow.
Phase
The direction of the arrow.
Probability, |ψ|²
Amplitude squared. The chance of finding the particle near a point. This link is called the Born rule.
Wavelength, λ
The distance over which the arrows make one full turn.
Momentum, p
Mass times velocity for a ball. For a wave, p = h/λ.
Energy of motion
Kinetic energy, p²/2m. Large where the wave curves sharply.
Energy of position, V(x)
Potential energy: what the particle has because of where it is. Hills, valleys, the pull of a nucleus.
Planck's constant, h and ħ
h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds, and ħ = h/2π. It links energy to frequency and momentum to wavelength.
Mass, m
How much matter the particle has. Heavier particles have shorter wavelengths and look more classical.
i
The square root of −1. Multiplying an arrow by i turns it a quarter turn.
∂, partial derivative
A rate of change with respect to one thing (time, or position) while the other is held fixed.
Superposition
Adding two wavefunctions, arrow by arrow, to get another valid one.
Interference
What adding does. Arrows pointing the same way reinforce, and opposite arrows cancel.
Stationary state
A wave shape whose arrows all turn together, so its probability pattern stays put. It has one definite energy.
Energy level
One of the allowed energies of a trapped particle.
Tunnelling
Getting through a barrier the particle does not have the energy to climb.
Measurement
Looking. It gives one definite result, at random, with odds set by |ψ|².
Uncertainty principle
A narrow packet needs a wide spread of momenta. Position and momentum cannot both be sharp.
